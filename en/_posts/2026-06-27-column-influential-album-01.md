# 1st (1st place)

## 1st Place Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band — The Studio as a New Instrument

London in 1967. The Beatles had already stopped touring and withdrew from the stage. That blank space was not just a pause, but a time to redesign the act of recording itself.

Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band is an album born as a result of this, with the concept of "a fictional live performance by a fictional band," and at the same time, it is a work that fundamentally rewrites the prerequisites for music production.

At the time, pop music was still dominated by a ""single-centered'' industrial structure. However, this work deviates from that structure and introduces a new concept of time, in which the entire album is designed as one work.

Recorded at EMI Studios (later Abbey Road Studios) in London, produced by George Martin. He brought the arrangement perspective of classical music to pop, integrating strings, brass, tape editing, reverse playback, and variable speed recording.

Particularly symbolic is the "disappearance of breaks" between songs. Audience applause, ambient sounds, and orchestral glissandi dissolve the gaps between songs, making the entire album function as one continuous stream of consciousness.

This structure became a reference point for later concept albums and established the concept of a ""work unit'' in rock music.

In terms of recording technology, countless tape dubbings were performed within the constraints of a 4-track recorder, creating artificial layers of sound. This "excessive density born from constraints" became the prototype for later studio productions.

Furthermore, as exemplified by ""A Day in the Life," the method of connecting different songwriting fragments through editing transformed traditional songwriting into ""the art of editing."

Culturally, it was perfectly synchronized with 1967's Summer of Love and became a symbol of psychedelic culture. However, what is important is that it was not just a reflection of the times, but functioned as an ""editor'' of the times themselves.

Rather than music reflecting society, music redesigns society's perceptual structure. This reversal is happening here.

The subsequent impact is direct. Progressive rock, art rock, and even modern pop album design are all under the influence of this work.

This album does not exist as a ""collection of songs," but as an ""edited conscious space."

## 2nd place Pet Sounds — Internalized pop structural revolution

1966, America's West Coast. Brian Wilson left the tour and confined himself in the studio to work on the album. The result was Pet Sounds.

Although this work is often described as a "response to the Beatles," it is actually much more complex than that. This work redesigned the very inner workings of pop music and became the blueprint for all subsequent studio music.

What is distinctive about this is that the organization of the rock band has almost been dismantled. Much of the actual performance is performed by studio musicians known as the Wrecking Crew, with the Beach Boys primarily serving as the vocal layer.

What is important here is the collapse of the concept of ""band = performance unit.'' What emerges in its place is a sound structure completely engineered by the producer.

Recording took place at a number of studios in Los Angeles, with the environments of Sanborn and Western Studio playing an especially important role. Brian Wilson considered the composition based on the "color of sound" and designed the music based on acoustic density rather than harmony.

The instruments used are also unique. It incorporates sound materials that deviate from pop grammar, such as percussion that sounds like the sound of hitting a salmon can, bells, cello, and the theremin-like tone of the ondes martenot.

The result is "structural music for emotion." The hooks and riffs of traditional hit songs have been diluted, and instead the emotional flow itself is structured.

This album became the starting point for later indie pop, dream pop, and even ambient pop. This is a turning point in music's transition from an ""extroverted expression" to an ""inner construction."

Brian Wilson's mental stress during the production process was later talked about as a symbol of the relationship between creativity and isolation.

His approach became the prototype for later producer-driven music production, anticipating the modern studio artist.

The Velvet Underground & Nico, released in March 1967, was a commercial failure at the time of its release. However, it is now regarded as one of the works that represents not only rock history but 20th century music as a whole.

## Evaluation at the time of release - an album whose time was too early

The Velvet Underground & Nico, released on March 12, 1967, was not exactly well-received.

The release label, Verve Records, is a prestigious label that handles a lot of jazz music, and they didn't have a clear strategy for how to market avant-garde rock music like this album.

Immediately after the album"s release, problems arose over the issue of Warhol"s ""Banana'' sticker on the album jacket and the rights of the person appearing in the photo on the back, which forced a temporary suspension of shipments.

As a result, many regions disappeared from the market without sufficient distribution.

In addition, this work had many elements that made it difficult for radio stations to broadcast it.

""Heroin" deals with drugs, ""Venus in Furs" deals with SM, and ""I'm Waiting for the Man'' depicts drug dealing.

Many songs were not eligible for airplay due to the high possibility of violating the Broadcasting Code.

Commercial results were also poor, with initial sales being extremely limited.

Although it briefly ranked in the album charts at the time, it was far from a big hit and could not be called a success in the eyes of the record company.

However, what is interesting is that the reactions of critics were also very divided.

While some critics gave it a negative review, calling it ""uncomfortable," ""the performance is shoddy," and ""the lyrics are decadent," there were also a few who praised it highly, saying it ""showed a new direction for rock."

In other words, it"s not that this album wasn"t understood, but that ""very few people could understand it.''

## It became a legend because it "didn"t sell"

In the 1970s, this album became a secret topic of conversation among used record stores and music lovers.

A famous quote by music producer Brian Eno symbolizes this.

> "The first album only sold 30,000 copies. But all 30,000 of them started a band."

There are various theories about this statement, and no clear primary source material has been confirmed as to where Eno actually used this expression.

However, the reason this phrase continues to be widely quoted is that it is, in fact, a very accurate expression of the historical impact of the work.

The young musicians who listened to this work received a new sense of value: ""The performance doesn"t have to be perfect," ""You can create music even if you don"t have a big studio," and ""It's okay to sing songs other than existing themes.''

In other words, this album is not a work that teaches ""how to be successful.''

It was a work that spread the idea that ""you can express yourself freely.''

## Album that made "punk" before punk

When punk rock emerged in the late 1970s, many of its core figures were directly influenced by the Velvet Underground.

Patti Smith inherited Lou Reed's literary lyricism and fused New York poetry with rock.

The Ramones established the basic form of punk rock by developing simple, short song structures and repetitive rhythms.

Television further pushed the possibilities of urban tension and guitar ensembles.

Furthermore, from the 1980s onwards, its influence became even more widespread.

*Sonic Youth fuses noise and alternative rock.
* R.E.M. established an indie rock aesthetic.
* The Jesus and Mary Chain brought feedback noise to the fore.
* Galaxie 500 has a minimalist performance and a deeper floating feeling.
* My Bloody Valentine elevated the roaring guitar to an artistic expression and laid the foundation for shoegaze.

Although these bands differ in their musical style, they share a common position in the lineage of the Velvet Underground in their attitude of ""rewriting the rules of existing rock.''

## The origin of the independent spirit

This work's influence on music history goes beyond its sound.

It is also extremely important that he rooted in rock the idea that ""selling" and ""expressing" are not necessarily the same thing.

The later formation of indie label culture, the DIY spirit, and the underground scene cannot be separated from the values ​​expressed by this album.

This lack of prioritizing commercial success is a continuum of 1980s indie rock, 1990s alternative, and 21st century bedroom music and independent production culture in the digital age.

Today, the cultural environment in which countless artists from all over the world are able to freely exhibit their work in small-scale environments resonates deeply with the idea of ​​""freedom of expression'' that this work represents.

> "'The Velvet Underground & Nico" was not a hit. However, its influence went far beyond sales and expanded rock from "music" to "a means of expression'. Its innovation is the biggest reason why it ranks third in this ranking."

## 4th place What's Going On (1971)

### An album that turned "love" into politics in an era when the world is broken.

What"s Going On, released on May 21, 1971, didn"t just change the history of soul music.

This work was one of the biggest turning points in 20th century music, determining the value that ""pop music can speak about society.''

At a time when hit songs up until then had centered around love and dance, this album focused on the themes of real life: war, racism, police violence, environmental destruction, poverty, faith, and family.

Moreover, it was not a sermon, but an enveloping expression with an overwhelmingly beautiful melody and smooth groove.

After this work, Seoul gained a new status as not just entertainment, but as ""art that reflects society.''

---

## Turbulent American society

Around 1970, America was in the midst of one of the greatest social unrests of the post-war era.

The Vietnam War turned into a quagmire, many young people were drafted into the military, and returning soldiers were left with deep physical and mental scars.

In 1968, Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated, followed by Robert F. Kennedy.

The civil rights movement was entering a new phase, with riots occurring one after another in urban areas.

Furthermore, there was a growing sense of crisis over pollution and environmental contamination caused by industrialization, and people began to question what they were losing in exchange for ""abundance.''

Against this backdrop, music has come to be expected to play a social role.

However, at the time, Motown's main product was sophisticated pop-soul.

Works with strong political overtones were considered to have little marketability, and the artists affiliated with the group were often asked to write songs with love themes.

Marvin Gaye directly objected to this policy.

---

## What changed Marvin Gaye

In the 1960s, Marvin Gaye was a Motown star with numerous hits including "I Heard It Through the Grapevine."

However, around 1970, he was suffering from severe mental distress.

One of the major factors was the death of singer Tammi Terrell, with whom she was close both in public and private life.

Terrell, who performed with the band for many years, died in 1970 at the age of 24 after battling brain cancer.

That sense of loss led Marvin Gaye to at one point refuse to even appear on stage.

It was also important that his younger brother, Frankie Gaye, returned from the Vietnam War and told of his harrowing experiences on the battlefield.

His testimony strongly instilled in Marvin Gaye the awareness that ""war is not a heroic tale, but a reality that destroys human beings.''

At the same time, police violence against black people, the civil rights movement stagnated, and poverty issues were looming as familiar realities.

He begins to feel strongly uncomfortable about ""continuing to sing only about love.''

---

## Background of the birth of the title song

The inspiration for ""What's Going On'' came from Renaldo Benson (Four Tops)' experience witnessing violent suppression of protests by police.

Benson felt, ""Why doesn't anyone sing about this situation?'' and began writing songs that looked at society.

Later, composer Al Cleveland joined the band, and eventually Marvin Gaye himself rewrote the lyrics, developing the song to have a more universal perspective.

"What"s Going On" in the title is more than just a question, "What"s going on?"

There are

"Why do we keep fighting each other?"

It contains a question for all of humanity.

## Recording studio and innovative production methods

The album was recorded primarily at Hitsville U.S.A. Studio A (Detroit) and a studio in Los Angeles, where Motown was relocating.

Motown's talented group of studio musicians, The Funk Brothers, participated in the production, and their outstanding playing skills supported the overall sense of unity of the work.

Unusually for the time, Marvin Gaye recorded his vocals not just once, but multiple times.

The reason for this was a coincidence.

When the engineer accidentally played different takes at the same time, Marvin Gaye liked the sound and asked to keep both.

This double vocal method created a unique floating feeling, as if the main melody and free fakes were having a conversation, and became the iconic sound of this work.

Furthermore, the structure that connects songs with almost no interruptions was groundbreaking.

Each song is not an independent single, but flows continuously as a single story.

This structure would have a major influence on later concept albums.

## "One prayer" called a concept album

What"s essential when talking about ""What"s Going On'' is its structure.

An album is not just a collection of songs.

It is designed to be a story about a veteran returning home, looking at a changed society, and thinking about human conflict, environmental destruction, faith, family, and hope.

The opening song, ""What's Going On," begins with the call, ""Brother, brother, brother," demonstrating the need for dialogue that transcends race and position.

The following song, ""What's Happening Brother,'' is told from the perspective of a soldier returning from the battlefield, expressing his confusion about his homeland, where both work and society have changed.

""Flyin' High (In the Friendly Sky)" deals with drug addiction, ""Save the Children" deals with responsibility for future generations, and ""Mercy Mercy Me (The Ecology)'' deals with the crisis of environmental destruction.

In 1971, hit albums that dealt directly with environmental issues were extremely rare.

""Mercy Mercy Me'' expressed concerns about air pollution, oil pollution, and radiation, and resonated with the growing environmental movement that began that year.

The album's final song, "Inner City Blues (Make Me Wanna Holler)," expresses with quiet anger the contradictions of American society, such as urban poverty, tax burdens, and tensions with the police.

And it doesn't end in complete despair.

Throughout the album, there is a sense of hope that resembles a prayer: ""Love can change the world.''

## Evaluation at the time of release and historical success

The album received high praise immediately after its release in May 1971.

Following the title song, ""Mercy Mercy Me (The Ecology)" and ""Inner City Blues (Make Me Wanna Holler)" were also hits, achieving great commercial success.

What is noteworthy is that its success overturned the conventional wisdom in the music industry at the time that ""works that deal with social issues do not sell.''

Critics hailed it as Marvin Gaye's breakthrough from soul singer to all-around artist.

This album also gave Motown artists greater creative freedom, allowing them to become more actively involved in album production.

The idea of ​​having the artists themselves construct the overall theme and sound of their work, rather than being led by a record company, had a major influence on subsequent black music as a whole.

---

## Rewritten the future of black music

Even before ""What's Going On,'' there were songs with social themes.

However, they were often released as singles.

This work was groundbreaking in that it depicted a single social message throughout the album.

This approach was inherited by many later artists.

Stevie Wonder's works, such as Music of My Mind, Innervisions, and Songs in the Key of Life, combine personal expression with social themes.

Curtis Mayfield further pushed the social nature of Soul with sharper depictions of urban realities and black communities.

From the 1980s onward, Prince developed an artist-driven creative approach to producing albums that crossed genres.

Furthermore, in the era of hip-hop, this spirit was inherited in new forms.

Public Enemy, 2Pac, Kendrick Lamar, and others have taken social inequality and racial issues as central themes in their works, and have continued the ""dialogue with society through music'' that this work opened up into the present day.

In addition, neo-soul standard-bearers such as D'Angelo, Erykah Badu, and Maxwell were greatly influenced by this work in their approach of constructing the entire work as a single idea.

What"s Going On isn"t a work that changed rock history.

However, it is an album that fundamentally changed the way music speaks about society.

Rather than shouting political statements, she was extremely innovative in encouraging people to have a dialogue with her compassionate voice and sophisticated sound.

Furthermore, the fact that it set a precedent for artists to express their ideas in the form of albums without being influenced by the intentions of record companies increases its historical value.

Today, expressing social issues and personal beliefs throughout a work is commonplace in many genres.

One of the works that built this cultural foundation is ""What's Going On.''

> "'What's Going On' confronts soul music with society and sublimates the album format into a single ideology. Can music change the world? It is a historical masterpiece that answers the question with beauty and empathy, and is worthy of being ranked 4th in this ranking."

## 5th place Kind of Blue

### An album that rewrote the future of jazz in "just two days"

Kind of Blue, released on August 17, 1959, is the best-selling jazz album in history, and one of the most influential works in all of music history.

This work is not just a masterpiece.

It was a revolutionary work that completely overturned the conventional wisdom of jazz performance and changed the way of thinking from ""performances that follow chord progressions" to ""performances that sing freely within a scale (mode)."

With this single album, the new concept of mode jazz permeated the world, and it had an immeasurable influence not only on subsequent jazz, but also on rock, fusion, ambient, film music, and contemporary music.

Despite its innovation, it only took two days to record.

---

## Turning point in 1950s jazz

Established in the 1940s by Charlie Parker and Dizzy Gillespie, bebop was characterized by fast, complex chord progressions and a high degree of improvisation.

Although hard bop and cool jazz later developed, many performers were still required to improvise within complex chord progressions.

Miles Davis begins to feel the limits of this situation.

He believed that by changing the chords one after another, the performer was forced to concentrate on following the harmony, and the room to freely sing the melody itself was limited.

He sought a wider musical space and was searching for new ways to improvise.

The hint was the modal theory proposed by composer and theorist George Russell.

Russell published ""Lydian Chromatic Concept of Tonal Organization'' in 1953, presenting a musical theory centered on modes, rather than the conventional idea centered on harmony.

Miles Davis is deeply interested in this idea, and envisions ""Kind of Blue'' as an album that puts it into practice.

---

It's not just the theory that makes Kind of Blue special.

The members who participated were some of the best in jazz history.

* Miles Davis (trumpet)

* Julian 'Cannonball' Adderley (alto saxophone)
* Bill Evans (piano)
* Wynton Kelly ("Freddie Freeloader" only)

In later years, performers who would each become masters representing the history of jazz gathered in one studio.

In particular, the presence of Bill Evans was decisive.

He was strongly influenced by impressionist music such as Claude Debussy and Maurice Ravel, and had a clear harmonic sense that was lacking in traditional jazz.

The sound perfectly matched Miles Davis's goal of ""music with spaces.''

## Why we hardly rehearsed

The biggest feature of this work is its thorough emphasis on the ""moment of playing for the first time''.

Rather than handing out completed scores, Miles Davis only gave the members a quick sketch right before the performance.

There is no detailed arrangement written there,

*mode
* Song flow
* Rough structure

Only was written.

In other words, performers had to ""discover'' music on the spot.

In later years, Bill Evans talked about this production method in his liner notes, comparing it to Japanese ink painting.

Like calligraphy or ink painting done with a single stroke, improvisation is a one-time act that cannot be modified, and the state of mind at that moment becomes the work itself.This idea runs throughout this work.

Therefore, the recording was not overly rehearsed.

Rather than perfection, priority was placed on the tension and natural dialogue that comes with creating sounds for the first time.

---

## Sounds of Columbia 30th Street Studio

Recording took place over two days, March 2 and April 22, 1959, at Columbia 30th Street Studio.

This studio was a renovated building originally built as a church, and was known for its high ceilings and rich reverberations.

It was an environment in which the original sound of the instrument spread naturally throughout the space without relying on artificial reverb.

Fred Plaut, who served as the recording engineer, took advantage of its acoustic characteristics to faithfully record the distance and atmosphere of each instrument.

Although it was recorded on analog tape, which was common at the time, editing was kept to a minimum, with many performances being recorded on the first take or only a few takes.

As a result, the album as a whole retains a natural feel, as if you were listening to a live session in front of your eyes, rather than a studio work.

## "Design of freedom" called mode

The core of ""Kind of Blue'' lies in the idea of ​​modal improvisation.

In traditional jazz, chord progressions change minutely, and improvisations are built on top of that. In other words, the performer had to constantly anticipate the ""next chord'' while assembling a phrase.

However, in modal jazz, a certain scale space is maintained for a long time. In this way, the performer can concentrate on the ""color changes within the scale" rather than ""the movement of chords".

As a result, improvisation was freed from competitive techniques and changed to expressions more like singing or speaking.

This structure is not just a theory; it determines the atmosphere of the album as a whole.

For example, in ""So What,'' the same mode is maintained for a long time, with the bass and horns continuing to interact.

""Blue in Green'' focuses on shifting sounds rather than a clear chord progression, creating the feeling that time itself is slowly dissolving.

## The recording scene that changed the world in 2 takes

Recording Kind of Blue was surprisingly simple.

The sessions took place over just two days, and most of the songs were recorded in a state close to how they were first heard.

Miles Davis did not prepare a detailed arrangement before entering the studio, and only gave the members very simple instructions.

Although this method may seem haphazard at first glance, it is actually a highly designed ""aesthetics of constraints.''

While giving freedom to the performers, by setting the framework of a mode, they were able to converge the infinite options into a certain direction.

Bill Evans later said of this production method, ""The work itself was not the finished painting, but the moment it was painted.''

As those words suggest, ""Kind of Blue'' was an album that recorded the process itself, rather than the resulting music.

---

## Extended influence from jazz history to "music history"

The influence of this work goes beyond the jazz genre.

From the late 1960s onwards, ""modal thinking'' spread in the fields of rock, film music, and electronic music.

The Doors took a modal approach to their long improvisational structures, expanding the song's sense of time itself.

In film music, it became common to create psychological tension by maintaining minimal harmony.

Furthermore, in fusion from the 1970s, Herbie Hancock and Wayne Shorter developed modal ideas and blurred the boundaries between jazz and rock.

Today, even in ambient and electronic music, the ""musical space without chord progressions'' has become a standard method.

This album is one of the origins of that.

---

## Re-evaluation and "Jazz Textbook"

When it was first released, Kind of Blue wasn't necessarily a huge hit.

However, its reputation gradually increased from the late 1960s, and by the 1970s it was widely recognized as a standard introduction to jazz.

Later, in the CD era, it was repeatedly reissued and established itself as the best-selling album in jazz history.

However, this ""textbook conversion'' also creates a certain contradiction.

Originally, this work was a record of free improvisation, music that refused to be confined by theory or form.

Despite this, it continues to be analyzed in later generations as a ""textbook of mode jazz.''

In other words, this album

"The freest music has become the most structured music."

It is also an existence with a paradox.

---

## Why is it ranked 5th?

""Kind of Blue" can be described as a ""reinvention of language" in the history of music.

However, in this ranking, it is positioned not as a work that destroys the structure itself, but as a work that expands on the inside of the structure.

The shift from bebop to mode was revolutionary, but still within the framework of jazz.

In contrast, the top works redefine the boundaries of genres, social structures, and the relationship between music and culture.

However, the idea of ​​""design for freedom'' presented by Kind of Blue became the premise for all subsequent musical improvisation.

> "'Kind of Blue' is a work designed with a minimal structure to maximize the freedom of improvisation. Its quiet innovation went beyond jazz and changed the way we think about music itself. Therefore, we rank it 5th in this ranking."

## 6th place Nevermind

### The moment when "the last rock revolution" destroyed pop culture

Nevermind, released on September 24, 1991, was more than just a hit.

It was a turning point that disrupted the "commercial rock order" that had lasted until the 1980s and pushed indie culture into the mainstream.

After this album, the center of the music industry changed dramatically.

LA's hard rock scene and MTV-style star system quickly lost influence, and Seattle-based grunge rose to the center of global pop culture in its place.

This work was a symbol of that.

---

## The "invisible city" of Seattle

The American music scene in the late 1980s was leaning toward extreme commercialization.

Glamorous hair metal, flashy PVs, and excessive production.

However, behind the scenes, a completely different music was growing.

That's the underground scene in Seattle, Washington.

The rainy and gloomy climate, isolated geographical conditions, and distance from major metropolitan areas created a unique musical culture.

At the center of this was Nirvana, with vocalist/guitarist Kurt Cobain.

They initially debuted on the indie label Sub Pop and were only a local presence.

However, the music was already packed with elements that would disrupt traditional rock.

---

## Definitive change in producer and sound

Butch Vig was the producer of this work.

He had the sense of balance to create a commercially viable sound image while retaining the roughness of an indie style.

Recording was primarily done at Sound City Studios.

This studio is known for its thick drum sound and is the place where many rock classics were created.

In particular, Dave Grohl's drums acquired an extremely strong sense of attack and spatial expanse by recording here.

The guitar was intentionally distorted and mixed to emphasize the low range.

This is an approach that is the complete opposite of the ""clear, well-organized 80's sound'' that had been mainstream up until then.

---

## Pop album as "anti-pop"

The greatest feature of Nevermind lies in its contradictions.

Although it had a catchy melody at first glance, the sound was extremely rough, the structure simple, and the lyrics unstable and introspective.

Their signature song, ""Smells Like Teen Spirit,'' has been hailed as a youth anthem around the world, but its lyrics are full of irony and weariness.

Kurt Cobain has often said that he "never intended to write a hit song," but the song ended up dominating MTV and radio.

This phenomenon became a classic example of underground culture being absorbed into the mainstream.

## Structural changes in the music industry

The success of Nevermind wasn"t just the band"s breakthrough.

It changed the structure of the entire music industry.

The previously dominant hair metal scene rapidly declined, and many bands lost their label contracts.

In its place, grunge and alternative rock entered mainstream markets, and major labels such as Geffen Records began to actively incorporate indie sounds.

Furthermore, MTV's music programs were forced to change direction from focusing on visuals to ""realistic images of bands.''

This trend would reshape the music business model for the entire 1990s.

---

## The contradiction that is Kurt Cobain

Kurt Cobain had a strong sense of discomfort with success itself.

He was originally an artist with a punk and hardcore spirit, and was not aiming for commercial success.

However, ""Nevermind'' quickly became a worldwide hit, expanding to a scale beyond his control.

This ""runaway success'' deepens his inner conflict.

It was this incompatibility between fame and alienation, influence and self-denial that cast a great shadow over his later works and life.

---

## Influence on subsequent music

The influence of ""Nevermind'' extends throughout rock from the 1990s onward.

* Post-grunge
* Alternative rock
* Emo
* Indie rock

Genres like this have expanded rapidly.

Some bands, such as Foo Fighters, rearranged the structure of pop after grunge.

In addition, the number of artists aiming for more introspective expressions is increasing, and values ​​that place more emphasis on ""emotional reality" than ""perfect performance" are becoming established.

---

## Why is it ranked 6th?

""Nevermind" is a work that can be called ""the last great rock revolution" in music history.

However, the essence of this is not the creation of a genre, but the ""reorganization of the existing rock industry''.

While their top works changed the very language of music or the very structure of culture, Nevermind changed the era by renewing the core of pop-rock.

Nevertheless, the destructive power of this work is overwhelming.

> "'Nevermind' is a work that brought the indie spirit to the mainstream and reset the commercial structure of rock once and for all. Its impact became a prerequisite for music culture from the 1990s onwards, and it is ranked 6th in this ranking."

## 7th place The Dark Side of the Moon

### "An album that converts the very inner thoughts of humans into sound"

The Dark Side of the Moon, released in 1973, redefined the concept of "perfection" in rock history.

Before that, rock music focused on performance, lyrics, and structure. However, this album integrates all of these and steps into the realm of ""designing life itself with sound.''

The theme is both extremely universal and abstract.

* time
* Money
* Madness
* death
* Labor
* Alienation

These are not individual song themes, but are designed as a "single consciousness structure" that runs through the entire album.

---

## A "laboratory" called a studio

Recording took place at Abbey Road Studios, with engineer Alan Parsons leading the sound design.

What is important here is that the recording was not just a record, but a "construction."

* Time distortion due to tape loops
* Multi-layer arrangement of clock sounds
* Ambient sound collage
* Space generation using analog synth

These are all ""rearrangements of real sounds'' and are not performance-centered structures like traditional rock.

## 8th place Autobahn

### "The moment the machine started playing music"

Autobahn, released in 1974, marked the "end of anthropocentrism" in music history.

Kraftwerk intentionally eliminated the ""human performance," ""emotional expression," and ""improvisation'' that had been the premise of rock up until then.

What was presented instead was a mechanical rhythm and a structured sound system.

---

## "Music" on the expressway

The album's centerpiece, ""Autobahn,'' is about German highways.

However, this is not a landscape depiction.

This is an attempt to musicalize the ""phenomenon of movement itself''.

* Constant speed repetitive rhythm
* Mechanical tone change
* minimal melody
* Long-term structure maintenance

Through these, music changes from "development" to "sustained."
