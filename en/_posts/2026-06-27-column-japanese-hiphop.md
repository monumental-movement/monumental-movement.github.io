## The dawn of Japanese hip-hop and "incidental moments" (1980s-early 1990s)

Text: mmr｜Theme: From subculture to mainstream, the evolutionary history of Japanese rap and the intersection of urban culture


### The impact of street culture and its arrival in Japan

The reception of hip-hop in Japan in the 1980s was not just an import of a musical genre, but an ""importation of physical expression and urban culture.'' Breakdancing, DJing, and rap were introduced in sets, creating a "complex culture" that did not exist in the Japanese music context at the time.

Particularly in Harajuku and Shibuya in Tokyo, dance teams gathered on the streets, and hip-hop as a visual culture took the lead in spreading.

### The appearance of DJ Krush and "sound abstraction"

One of the important turning points in the history of Japanese hip-hop was the emergence of DJ Krush. He transcended the boundaries of rap and treated the beat itself like "environmental music."

In the club scene, there was a trend where atmosphere was more important than words, and Japanese rap began to be redefined as ""music of voices.''

### Trial and error of early rap and the reality of the city

Rappers at the time were mixing English phrases with Japanese, searching for forms of language that fit the rhythm. The Japanese language's ""length of word endings" and ""many vowels" were major constraints on flow construction, but at the same time they were also a source of creativity.

>Japanese hip-hop at this time existed not as a genre but as an "urban experimental device"

## 90s: Awakening of Japanese rap and "incidental works"

### King Giddra and the social explosion

In the mid-1990s, Japanese rap suddenly began to take on a social aspect. The symbol of this is King Giddra. Their song ""Power from the Sky'' was a historical work that verbalized the feeling of confinement in the city and the anger of young people.

The composition of Zeebra, K Dub Shine, and DJ Oasis was "polyphonic hip hop", each depicting the city from a different perspective.

In particular, K Dub Shine's lyrics brought a "criticism" to Japanese rap with a style that intersected news perspectives and personal feelings.

### Scha Dara Parr's humor and everydayness

Around the same time, Scha Dara Parr was instrumental in reducing hip-hop to the language of everyday life. In contrast to heavy social criticism, his weapons were light play on words and a sense of life.

Through this contrast, Japanese rap acquires two axes: ""anger" and ""play."

### Impact of Buddha Brand and "Human Power Plant"

In the underground world, Buddha Brand's ""Human Power Plant'' becomes a symbolic incident. This song is said to be a work that established the "black aesthetic" of Japanese rap.

Their heavy bass, smoky beats, and unique sense of language had a strong impact on the club scene at the time.

## 2000s: Majorization and birth of battle culture

### Mainstreaming Zeebra and Expanding Hip-Hop

Zeebra became a symbol of the mainstreaming of Japanese rap. They expanded their activities from clubs to television, from the streets to advertising, and instantly boosted hip-hop's social recognition.

On the other hand, this "success" also created a distance from the underground.

### The birth of battle culture before the R-designation

In the early 2000s, MC battle events began to appear across Japan. In particular, the competitive style of improvisation transformed hip-hop from "music" to "language sport."

What was important here was that audiences began to appreciate not only the ""accuracy of the rhymes" but also the ""instantaneous storytelling."

### Rhymester and the establishment of intellectual rap

Rhymester established themselves in the 2000s as a rare band that could both be pop and critical. They created humor and social criticism at the same time, expanding the ""intellectual realm'' of Japanese rap.

## 2010s: Impact of internalization and trapping

### KOHH and urban loneliness

KOHH is a presence that brought out the ""individual inner world'' in Japanese rap in the 2010s. Rather than stories of success or the streets, his lyrics were built around loneliness, loss, and the fluctuations of everyday life.

This was a shift from the traditional "expression of strength" to "expression of vulnerability."

### CONTINUATION OF ANARCHY AND REALITY

ANARCHY was a rapper who continued to depict the reality of the streets, and even after his success, he continued to develop works centered on depicting reality.

His presence presented a perspective of ""coexistence with the streets" rather than ""escape from the streets."

### The Rise of Trap and BAD HOP

BAD HOP, based in Kawasaki, Kanagawa, presented a new model of Japanese rap by combining trap sounds with regional characteristics.

They quickly spread through YouTube, becoming both local and global at the same time.

## SoundCloud generation and "DIY urban music"

### Expansion of online artists

After SoundCloud, rap completely shifted to individual production. By releasing works without going through a studio or label, hip-hop changed from ""professional music" to ""an extension of life."

### Collapse of music genres

Hip-hop is merging with electro, rock, and pop, and clear genre boundaries are disappearing. This has led to an increase in the number of rappers who are also producers at the same time.

### A new meaning to freestyle

Battle culture has moved to YouTube, and while improvisation has become a "show," it has become even more important in terms of visualizing language ability.

## Modern times: Globalization and the rap of multi-tiered cities

### Awich and global connectivity

Hailing from Okinawa, Awich has become a symbol of the global connectivity of Japanese rap. Her music traverses Japanese, English, and Okinawan culture, presenting a multilayered identity.

### SNS and instant hit structure

Thanks to short-form media such as TikTok, rap has moved from the ""era of full albums" to the ""era of fragments." Structures in which choruses and passages are spread out independently become commonplace.

### Redefinition as an urban language

Today's Japanese hip-hop is not just music, but functions as an ""urban language system.'' It has a structure in which regional, individual, and global characteristics coexist at the same time.
